# LUNORIZON validation

Each check states what was compared, what was measured, what it supports and what it does not. Results files live in `data/validation/`. The scripts are in `pipeline/` and `app/tests/`.

## 1. Sun and Earth geometry vs JPL Horizons

- **Compared:** topocentric azimuth and elevation of the Sun and Earth from our DE440/SPICE ephemeris and our site frame, against the JPL Horizons API with an observer on the lunar surface (`coord@301`, airless apparent).
- **Cases:** 4 south-polar sites × 5 dates (2025–2031) × 2 bodies = 40.
- **Result:** maximum elevation difference 0.00003°, azimuth 0.00001° (`horizons-crosscheck.json`). The browser engine (TypeScript) passes the same comparison within 0.001° (`app/tests/engine.test.ts`).
- **Supports:** the geometry, frames, light time and parallax are correct.
- **Does not cover:** terrain.
- **Reproduce:** `python pipeline/validate_horizons.py`

## 2. Illumination vs a published study

- **Compared:** average visible solar disk, share of time with any Sun, and longest shadow, at 2 m above ground, hourly 2017–2037. The reference is Table 2 of Gläser et al. (2018), *Planet. Space Sci.* 162, co-authored at NASA GSFC (NTRS 20170007365).

| Site | Published avg / vis / longest shadow | LUNORIZON at point | LUNORIZON best within 100 m |
|---|---|---|---|
| Connecting Ridge C1 | 88.0% / 92.1% / 112 h | 84.9% / 89.6% / 268 h | 87.3% / 91.5% / 181 h |
| Connecting Ridge C2 | 78.1% / 82.0% / 165 h | 78.1% / 82.0% / 165 h | 78.1% / 82.0% / 165 h |
| Shackleton rim | 85.5% / 89.4% / 66 h | 84.0% / 89.3% / 67 h | 85.4% / 89.4% / 67 h |

- **Why "within 100 m":** the published maxima were found on a track-adjusted LOLA grid; ours is the public PDS grid. C1 is a sharp single peak, and its longest shadow is the most sensitive number. We report the difference rather than tune it away.
- **Why C1's longest shadow differs:** it is ill-conditioned. Within 100 m of C1 it ranges from 150 to 689 h; with the skyline 0.1° lower (the typical terrain-model error, section 7) the shortest within 100 m is 118 h, against the published 112 h. At the best point a ±0.1° skyline moves it between 175 and 207 h. At C2 the same test moves it only 164–166 h, and at Shackleton rim 63–69 h, both bracketing the published values. Average illumination is robust at all three; the longest shadow on a single peak should not be read to better than a factor of about two.
- **Reproduce:** `python pipeline/validate_glaser.py`

## 3c. Historical Deep Space Network proxy mask vs JPL Horizons

`pipeline/build_dsn.py` marks, for every hour, which of Goldstone (DSS-14), Madrid (DSS-63) and Canberra (DSS-43) sees the Moon's centre at 10° or more (antenna positions from DSN 810-005; Earth orientation IAU_EARTH from pck00011). `pipeline/validate_dsn.py` compares the Moon's elevation at the three antennas with JPL Horizons at 6 dates, 2025–2031: largest difference 0.074°, against a 10° mask. Over 2023–2031 at least one complex sees the Moon 98.3% of the time; the longest gap is 2 h.

This is retained as a historical comparison. The runtime now uses the named 34 m reference-antenna geometry described in the final section below, with moving ITRF93 positions and binary Earth PCKs. These historical percentages do not validate current station paths.

## 3d. Light-and-shadow map vs site horizons

The map's "this moment" layer marches the 80 m and 480 m terrain toward the Sun from every pixel (`bodyFractionAt` in `app/src/lib/terrain.ts`). At four polar sites (C1, Shackleton rim, Nobile Rim 2, Athena) and 200 random hours each in 2023–2031, its lit / dark call agrees with the site's own 20 m (or NAC) horizon 99.1% of the time (98–100% per site). The disagreements are hours when the Sun grazes the skyline, where the finer terrain decides.

## 4. Browser horizon vs pipeline

Horizons computed in the browser (80 m near field, 480 m far field) are compared with the pipeline horizons (20 m or NAC near field) at four curated sites. The median absolute skyline difference is 0.006–0.06° (`app/tests/engine.test.ts`).

Away from the pole the browser uses the 1.9 km whole-Moon grid (LDEM_16). Against the pipeline horizons (59 m LDEM_512 within 25 km, 474 m beyond) at the 11 non-polar sites:

| Site | median / max skyline difference |
|---|---|
| Apollo 11, 12, 14, 15, 16, 17 | 0.72/1.8°, 0.32/0.8°, 0.88/3.5°, 0.44/1.6°, 0.46/1.8°, 0.63/2.7° |
| SLIM, Chang'e 6, 4, 5, Blue Ghost | 0.49/2.5°, 0.07/4.1°, 0.10/1.8°, 0.02/1.3°, 0.16/1.8° |

The error comes from small craters and hills a 1.9 km grid cannot resolve. Near the equator the Sun climbs about 0.5° per hour, so 1° of skyline is about 2 h of sunlight. Sites added there are labelled as quick estimates. We tested a 948 m grid: it cuts the error at hilly sites by about half (Apollo 14 median 0.88° to 0.41°), barely helps on mare (Apollo 11 0.72° to 0.71°), and would add 70–100 MB to the static site, so we did not ship it.

Slopes for touchdown grids computed in the browser come from the 80 m grid over a 160 m baseline. At a3-mouton, nobile-dm2, a3-haworth, im2-actual and c1 the median slope over the same points is within 1° of the 40 m pipeline slope (5.6 vs 6.5°, 6.0 vs 6.1°, 6.0 vs 6.2°, 2.6 vs 2.9°, 5.5 vs 6.0°), but the share of points steeper than 10° is about half (6 vs 13%, 9 vs 14%, 1 vs 10%, 0 vs 0%, 16 vs 19%). The grid caption states this.

## 5. Artemis III availability vs NASA's stated logic

NASA's Artemis III site-selection team writes that "individual regions typically provide limited viable opportunities when all constraints are simultaneously satisfied … more than one landing region must be maintained" (George et al., LPSC 2026, NTRS 20250011660).

We tested this with LUNORIZON on 2027 landing days. For each of the 9 regions we used the best point found in its box (`pipeline/search_regions.py`). The requirements were a 6.25-day stay in continuous sunlight, Earth in view at least 50% of the time, and slope at most 8°.

- Over the 365 landing days from 7 Jan 2027, the best single region qualifies on 133 days.
- Keeping all nine open, at least one qualifies on 283 days (78%).
- With every skyline raised 0.1° (section 7), 279 days remain: the conclusion does not hinge on terrain-model error.
- Eclipses count as darkness: on 12 January 2028 Earth hides the whole Sun at Malapert for about an hour, so a "no darkness" crew stay that spans it fails. That one eclipse removed a day at Malapert (132 → 131) and six at Haworth.
- Reproduce: the "Artemis III: why NASA keeps nine regions" button on the page.

This is a qualitative reproduction of NASA's argument with our own model. It is not NASA's analysis: it omits launch, transfer and landing constraints.

## 6. Polar map layers vs independent recomputation

The sunlight and Earth-in-view map layers come from the Python pipeline (`build_illumination.py`, `build_earthvis_polar.py`). The test recomputes 8 random grid nodes within 150 km of the pole from scratch with the TypeScript engine (its own horizon from the 80 m / 480 m tiles, then a full 2027 hourly run). Layer minus engine: Earth in view −0.2 to +0.2 percentage points, sunlight −0.3 to +0.1 (`app/tests/engine.test.ts`).

At the curated points the map reads lower than the point itself: 0–1 points at the Athena and Odysseus sites, but 9–32 points less Earth time and 9–43 points less sunlight at the ridge, rim and Artemis region points. Those points were picked as local highs within their area, and a 1–2 km map cell averages them with lower ground. Use the map to find an area, and the site's own numbers for the point.

## 7. How far off is a LOLA skyline?

Every event time depends on the terrain skyline, so we measured its error where sharper terrain exists. At the Athena and Chandrayaan-3 sites the horizon was computed twice: with the LROC NAC stereo model as near field, and with LOLA alone (`pipeline/validate_skyline_error.py`, output in `data/validation/skyline-nac-vs-lola.json`).

| Site | Sharper terrain | LOLA | median / 90th percentile / max difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Athena (IM-2) | NAC NOBILE03, 4 m | 20 m | 0.003° / 0.066° / 0.26° |
| Chandrayaan-3 | NAC VIKRAMSITE1, 3 m | 240 m | 0.009° / 0.115° / 0.32° |

The page uses a ±0.1° skyline shift (about the 90th percentile) as a sensitivity test, not an error bound, and shows how far the first sunset moves for a skyline that much higher or lower. At the flown sites that is small: Odysseus ±1.4 h, Chandrayaan-3 ±0.6 h, Athena ±1.2 h, Blue Ghost ±0.2 h. Near the pole the Sun skims the skyline and the range grows: Nobile Rim 2's first nominally qualifying 2027 day (8 Jan) would land in darkness under a skyline 0.1° higher. Region default dates are therefore the first day that still qualifies with that margin (Nobile Rim 2: 9 Jan; the other eight were already robust), and the page flags any date you pick that passes only nominally. The same shift applied to every landing day of 2027 shows that most passing days are robust: 283 → 279 for the nine regions combined, Malapert 131 → 129, Haworth 69 → 65, Connecting Ridge C2 121 → 116 (each counted from its default date, Artemis III crew preset). The page reports both counts and flags a chosen date as fragile when only the nominal skyline passes it. The touchdown-error map reports the share of its 500 m circle that meets the requirements both ways too. For Haworth on its default date (11 Jan 2027, Artemis III crew rules) that is 53% nominal and 52% with the raised skyline at 2 m panel height, and 69% / 68% with 10 m panels; the map follows the page's panel-height switch (`app/tests/review.test.ts` pins these numbers). Two flat landing sites are a small sample, and the NAC models are themselves tied to LOLA, so this is an estimate, not a bound; section 7b shows polar ridges are harder. Sites you add north of 75°S use the 1.9 km whole-Moon grid, whose skyline differs from 59 m terrain by a median 0.02–0.9° and up to 4° (section 6), so their ranges use a 1° shift and say so; near the equator that is about ±2 h of sunset.

### 7b. Polar ridges and rims: what the difference does

`pipeline/validate_skyline_error.py` also compares three DLR 2 m stereo models at the south pole (LROC NAC DTMs `ESALL_CR1` Connecting Ridge, `ESALL_SR12` Shackleton rim, `ESALL_MP1` Malapert peak), at each model's centre and four random points within 1.5 km. Their heights sit about 1 m above LOLA 20 m (median absolute deviation about 1 m), so registration is not the issue. The skyline differs much more than at the flown sites: 90th percentile 0.5–1.5°, worst 4.6°, from metre-scale relief within a few hundred metres that a 20 m grid smooths away.

`pipeline/validate_skyline_effect.py` turns that into the numbers people use (15 points, all of 2027):

| | LOLA vs 2 m stereo |
|---|---|
| Annual share of time with any Sun | median difference 0.7 pp, largest 3.2 pp |
| Single sunsets (331 matched) | 77% within 1 h; 75th percentile 1 h; 90th percentile 7 h |
| Single sunsets at the 4 points with slope ≤ 10° | 75th percentile 9.8 h; 90th percentile 18 h |

- **Holds:** shares of sunlight and the maps built from them. This check does not test mission verdicts or availability counts; section 7c does.
- **Does not hold to the hour:** single sunset times on polar ridges, especially on flat crests, where the Sun skims along the ridge. The ±0.1° range understates this, so every polar headline says so, whether or not the ±0.1° range is wide.

### 7c. Do mission verdicts survive sharper terrain?

Average sunlight agreeing is not the same as a mission verdict agreeing: a 6.25-day crew stay fails if a single hour is dark. `pipeline/validate_mission_effect.py` checks every landing day of 2027 at 18 points: 15 random points in the three DLR stereo regions, Athena (4 m NOBILE03), and the actual curated C1 and Shackleton coordinates. Each terrain supplies its own 40 m baseline slope. All four rule presets include their slope threshold, Earth visibility at 0° clearance, and joint terrain/eclipse visibility; the DSN requirement is off.

The page's hourly LOLA horizons, quantized to the shipped 0.01°, are compared with 2–4 m stereo near-field horizons at 10-minute steps. A raised 0.1° LOLA horizon tests sensitivity. Separate hourly/10-minute runs isolate time-step effects. First terrain sunset is interpolated as in the app, including the right endpoint of the final hour without counting it as an extra hour; the independent eclipse test checks whether any part of the solar cap remains outside Earth's disk. An app regression reproduces all four nominal annual counts at C1 and Shackleton from the shipped assets.

Results (`data/validation/mission-effect.json`; 18 × 365 = 6,570 point-days per preset):

| Rules | Passing point-days, LOLA / stereo | LOLA pass, stereo fail | LOLA fail, stereo pass | LOLA passes confirmed | Raised passes confirmed | Annual days per point, median / max absolute difference | LOLA hourly/10-min flips |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Artemis III crew | 359 / 365 | 4 | 10 | 98.9% | 99.7% | 2 / 5 | 0 |
| No-night lander | 245 / 250 | 2 | 7 | 99.2% | 100.0% | 1 / 6 | 1 |
| Default (14-day stay) | 335 / 361 | 2 | 28 | 99.4% | 99.7% | 1 / 21 | 0 |
| Hardened (14-day stay) | 570 / 541 | 47 | 18 | 91.8% | 92.9% | 3 / 33 | 6 |

- **Interpretation:** crew-rule passes are confirmed in 355 of 359 cases in this sample, with passes at only 5 of the 18 points. Hardened rules have 47 false passes out of 570; neither one percentage nor the crew result validates every preset. Differences include terrain-specific slope as well as skyline differences. Nominal-only pass confirmation ranges from 72.7% to 92.3% across presets; the sensitivity flag is not a confidence interval.
- Annual-count medians and maxima above are taken over points with at least one pass in either terrain, rather than padded with the many points that never pass.
- **Limits:** the stereo products are tied to LOLA and clustered in three small DLR regions plus Athena. Adding two curated, well-lit points changes the distribution substantially; do not interpret pooled percentages as a chosen site's reliability. The JSON retains random/Athena and curated subsets. Most points never pass crew rules. Hourly-versus-10-minute comparisons can still miss shorter events, and neither represents flight safety, attitude or thermal survival.
- **On the page:** polar site limits and Evidence Check 4 show these denominators and the per-preset differences. The verdict names its actual requirements; power and landing safety require separate assessment.
- **Reproduce:** `python pipeline/validate_mission_effect.py`; independent geometry/slope regressions: `python -m unittest discover -s pipeline -p test_mission_effect.py`. Source terrain caches are required to rerun the full validation.

## 8. Eclipses: terrain and Earth hiding the same Sun

During an eclipse seen from the Moon, Earth can cover part of the Sun while the skyline covers another part, and the two parts can overlap. The visible share is the part of the disk that is both above the skyline and outside Earth's disk, integrated with adaptive Simpson quadrature on the sky plane, splitting at the skyline and disk-intersection boundaries (`visibleSunFraction` in `app/src/lib/astro.ts`). Every view uses it: window statistics, power, the 3D view, the touchdown map and the light-and-shadow map.

- **Checked against sampling:** at Shackleton rim on 12 Jan 2028 03:00 UTC the engine gives 29.1% of the disk visible; sampling 125,000 points on the solar disk against the site's real skyline and Earth's disk gives 29.0%. Multiplying the two separate remaining shares, as an earlier version did, gives 38.4%. Over the whole eclipse, every 15 minutes, engine and sampling agree within 2 points.
- **Light after an eclipse:** when an eclipse ends the darkness, the Sun reappears only once Earth uncovers a part of it the terrain does not hide, so neither event alone gives the time. The engine finds it by bisection within the hour. Landing at Shackleton at 04:00 UTC that day, light returns after 29 minutes, the same as a minute-by-minute scan (an earlier version said "0 min"). The headline says the lander arrives during an eclipse.
- **Reproduce:** `cd app && npx vitest run tests/review.test.ts`

## 9. Power and downlink together

These are model assumptions, not validations; no flight-measured lunar downlink rate was found to compare with.

- **Antenna gain depends on frequency.** A dish's gain is efficiency × (π D / λ)², with 55% efficiency: a 0.5 m dish is 30.3 dBi at X band (8425 MHz) and 19.0 dBi at S band (2295 MHz). Patch and omni antennas keep a fixed gain on either band. An earlier version kept 30 dBi for the dish on S band, overstating the S-band rate about 13.5 times.
- **The radio needs power.** While it transmits the radio draws 3.3 W from the bus per watt of RF output (a solid-state amplifier near 30% efficiency), added to the Power panel's load. No data flows while the battery is flat. The page shows the data home with this power and, separately, the link limit if the radio always had power. Its longest-silence headline also includes unpowered hours (including a partial final powered hour); the geometry-only gap is labelled separately. The brief uses the same values. Odysseus from 29 Feb 2024 23:00 UTC for 7 days, page defaults with reference-station geometry: the battery is flat after about 49.1 h, so about 73 GB comes home against a 255 GB link limit. The former centre/proxy baseline was 71 / 250 GB; neither is a flight-volume measurement.
- **A radio rate cap is optional.** The link budget gives the highest rate the link closes at. A real modem and its allotted bandwidth may cap it lower; the page has a "Max rate" field (off by default) and says when no cap is set.
- **Still upper bounds:** antenna scheduling, other DSN users and weather on Earth are not modelled.

## 10. Storage losses, operational limits and minimum rated capacity

Primary sources: NASA/CR-2020-220368 Table 14 and NASA/TM-20210020132 EPS assumptions/design. Their values describe particular concept studies. LUNORIZON's loss-aware example is independently chosen and explicitly labeled illustrative.

The state is stored energy above the protected floor. For rated capacity C, allowed depth of discharge D and initial SOC s0, usable storage is C D and initial usable energy is C max(0, s0 - (1-D)). Charging adds the smaller of surplus bus power and the charging limit, times charging efficiency. Delivering a bus deficit draws stored energy divided by discharge efficiency, subject to the discharge-output limit. Darkness-only heater demand and constant array derating are independent inputs.

The minimum capacity calculation uses storage-domain cumulative energy increments. Its required capacity is the larger of maximum cumulative drawdown / D and initial-prefix deficit / initially available fraction. A zero initially available fraction with an early cumulative deficit has no capacity-only remedy. A discharge-output bottleneck likewise returns no finite sizing remedy. Both nominal and raised-skyline calculations must pass after a proposed battery change is applied.

`app/tests/storage.test.ts` checks analytical cases, a 25-case independent subhour numerical integration/bisection comparison, and the conservation identity:

`generated + initial stored = demanded - unserved + final stored + storage losses + curtailed surplus`.

Specific checks include a 1,000 Wh battery with 80% allowed depth, 70% initial SOC and 80% discharge efficiency: 500 stored Wh are initially available; 400 Wh reach the bus; a 100 W load lasts four hours. A rated 1,250 Wh is sufficient for five hours, and 1,249 Wh is not. Another case has positive aggregate energy balance but insufficient rate-limited recharge, so it still fails. A power-limited battery can retain substantial charge while full bus demand and downlink fail. The ideal default reproduces existing numerical regression results.

URL tests cover every storage setting, uncapped versus disabled power limits, invalid model inputs and old six-field power links. Hourly CSV tests sum bus energy and powered data back to the simulation. These verify a mathematical constant-parameter storage model, not battery chemistry, thermal survival or measured flight performance.

## 11. General site/date search and powered service targets

`app/tests/search.test.ts` compares the search against explicit evaluation of all 21 pairs in a three-point, seven-day NASA-derived scenario. Each independent check constructs a raised horizon, reruns power/link, and applies the service thresholds. The nondominated result is separately checked by exhaustive pairwise dominance enumeration. Synthetic cases retain equivalent options and real conflicts while removing strictly dominated options.

Data-return filtering uses the smaller powered volume from the nominal/raised runs. Silence filtering uses the larger within-stay powered gap. Both mission rules and both power screens must pass before a pair is eligible. These are two sensitivity cases, not statistical lower/upper confidence bounds. Targets remain conditional on uninterrupted DSN antenna allocation during geometric contact.

Boundary tests account separately for stays outside the ephemeris. Duplicate sites are removed. Cancellation aborts between bounded chunks and does not publish an incomplete run as a completed result. Search inputs round-trip separately from the main viewed date. CSV retains every evaluated pair, failures and input assumptions. Date spacing limits completeness to the sampled grid; no launch/transfer solution or globally optimal continuous-time trajectory is inferred.

## 12. Contact allocation and finite payload delivery

Primary-source motivation: NASA's explanation of competing DSN allocations, the Madrid complex's pass definition, and Verville/Eddy's *Onboard Processing for LunaNet Data Services* (NTRS 20240012437). This implementation is an hourly planning/queue model, not a reproduction of their relay network or a DTN protocol stack.

`app/tests/communications.test.ts` checks complex masks, daily UTC slots wrapping midnight, exclusive end times, unioned absolute passes, no multiplied rate for overlapping complexes, and correct absolute times after a mission window is sliced. Transmitter DC demand is added only during selected, planned geometric contact. A missing DSN mask remains explicitly a terrain-only estimate; schedule controls do not create a real reservation.

Finite-buffer examples test simultaneous arrival and transmission, initial overcapacity, zero-buffer direct forwarding, and acquisition/transmission suppression during power loss. Thirty independently fine-stepped input/contact/power histories agree with the analytical interval queue. Initial and produced data are conserved across delivered, pending and discarded quantities. Minimum no-overflow buffer uses the same powered input and reserved passes. A one-bit numerical tolerance applies when comparing the modeled loss to its explicit limit.

Real-data regressions show that a lit, powered de Gerlache point can fail the mission screen solely because a zero-hour daily allocation causes payload overflow. Search does not accept that pair even with a zero minimum return target. Share tests preserve all radio/operations/traffic values, including station selection and absolute dates. CSV round-trips contact windows and rejects invalid UTC dates, fractional-hour boundaries, reversed intervals and unknown complexes without partial application.

The browser workflow checks zero-allocation overflow, visible headline warning, search exclusion, Mission brief, valid and invalid contact-plan imports, plan export and fresh-context restoration. Contact controls and full date fields are inspected at 1263x560, 1440x900 and 390x844. Capacity and payload columns are explicitly distinguished in hourly export metadata. Nominal Mission brief results now include power and enabled data loss; its annual heatmap counts and raised-skyline statement are explicitly limited to Sun/Earth/slope rules.

## 13. Earth-station geometry gap diagnostic

`pipeline/fetch_kernels.py` downloads the five named NAIF releases used by `ephemeris.py`; the committed `data/validation/kernels.json` records source URLs, byte counts and SHA-256 checksums. `--verify` confirms the local cache without downloading. Raw kernels remain ignored. Source refresh is explicit via `--refresh-manifest` and should be reviewed rather than treated as an automatic recalibration.

`pipeline/probe_station_geometry.py` evaluates six shipped sites hourly during January 2027. It compares the browser's Earth-centre LT+S vectors with NAIF `spkcpt` XLT+S transmission directions for the existing DSS-14/63/43 coordinate proxies, retaining the shipped terrain and ground-complex mask. This isolates a practical modeling gap, not an independent truth measurement. It also includes the difference between reception and transmission corrections. Results and sample timestamps are in `data/validation/station-geometry-gap.json`.

For example, Connecting Ridge C1 at 2027-01-09 21:00 UTC has modeled Earth-centre clearance +0.02307 degrees but DSS-14-target clearance -0.69899 degrees. The six-point diagnostic has 70 station-hours where centre clearance passes and station-target clearance fails, and 32 with the opposite result, among 5,460 eligible station-hours. These are clustered model-comparison counts, not a reliability rate or an independent validation claim. Implementing and independently checking exact antenna target directions remains open. Individual 34 m station positions, measured Earth-orientation parameters and configuration-dependent gain/noise are not provided by this diagnostic.

## Equipment-node heat balance and shared electrical supply

The optional `PowerSystem.thermal` scenario evaluates one isothermal node, not the lunar surface or a complete spacecraft. All 15 fields are explicit user assumptions: initial/min/max/setpoint K, heat capacity J/K, effective Sun-facing projected area/solar absorptivity, radiating area/emissivity, ground IR view factor/fixed K, mounting conductance/fixed K, retained bus waste-heat fraction and maximum heater W. Example values are illustrative and not a NASA vehicle specification. A disabled scenario does not infer temperature feasibility.

The heat equation is `C dT/dt = α Aprojected S visibleDisk + ηheat (Pbase + PradioDC − PradioRF) + Pheater − ε σ Arad [T⁴ − F Tground⁴ − (1−F) 3⁴] − G(T−Tmount)`. Outgoing radio RF energy is excluded before crediting retained equipment heat; base load is assumed dissipative, and the retained fraction represents other unmodeled distribution/external work. Sun distance and per-site visible disk follow shared geometry. Projected area is assumed constant toward visible Sun; no attitude geometry is inferred. The radiation/view-factor formulation is consistent with [NASA Passive Thermal Control Engineering Guidebook v5.1](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20220006584), §4.2.3 p.44, which was visually inspected. That reference does not validate the chosen equipment geometry or parameters.

Heater commands update once per ephemeris hour and hold through it. Demand targets the setpoint, capped at the selected maximum, and replaces `darkHeaterW`. Electricity shares the same storage/rate/loss model as base demand and radio. The model credits internal heat and heater dissipation only during the full-bus powered prefix of an hour; direct solar absorption persists while unpowered. Actual partially delivered bus energy may heat real hardware, but no such distribution or load shedding is inferred here. Reported actual/counterfactual temperatures reflect this explicit convention. A limit breach marks a failed scenario; the model continues conditional loads/rates and does not infer a temperature-dependent device shutdown or recovery.

Heat integration uses backward Euler/Newton with step doubling. A trial step is at most 300 s; one-step and two-half-step results must differ by no more than `0.00001 + 1e-7 max(Tstart,Tend)` K. The accepted two half steps preserve monotonicity under constant forcing and the implicit solve handles short thermal time constants. This local numerical tolerance is not a global physical uncertainty bound. A solver/work-limit failure raises an error, not a passing temperature result. Temperature-limit crossings are interpolated on accepted integration steps. Display and hourly CSV sample hour boundaries and are not the full integration trajectory.

`pipeline/validate_thermal_node.py` creates `data/validation/thermal-node-reference.json` using pinned SciPy 1.15.3 DOP853 (`rtol=1e-11`, `atol=1e-10`) with dense-output root solving for cold/hot durations. Six separate cases cover radiation, warm ground, conduction, combined losses, a stiff small node and isolated heating. `tests/thermal.test.ts` checks terminal values, 169 retained trajectory samples and durations against this separate continuous-ODE implementation; maximum terminal error is 0.01485 K, every retained trajectory sample differs by <0.2 K, and duration outside limits differs by <30 s. Shorter maximum-step runs converge. The discrete heat-balance residual is also checked (<0.01 J in these cases). Additional extreme allowed-input tests stay positive/monotone and approach the 3 K / 1000 K boundary equilibria, with <1 J accumulated equation residual. These criteria validate only the selected equation/solver over these cases, not arbitrary real hardware or future environmental accuracy.

Further tests verify analytic linear heating and conductive equilibrium, radiation signs/view factors, caps, initial violations, crossing durations, replacement of darkness-only heating, electrical energy accounting, heater/internal-heat loss after bus depletion, full-power thermal failures and the absence of a capacity remedy for those failures, discharge-power constraints, shared downlink interruption, and a capacity bisection with losses and partial initial charge. ## Conditional two-hop service-plan accounting

At each bin, backhaul first serves relay backlog already present at bin start. Access arrivals are then admitted at bin end, so they cannot reach Earth in that bin, even when contacts overlap.
